# Visual Storytelling Workflow

Creating a visual narrative that connects the product to a concept and communicates it through imagery, motion, color, and copy alignment.

---

# Phase 1: Define the Story

Step 1 — Write the product in one concrete sentence.
Not a tagline. Not a pitch. A factual description: "This product monitors social media conversations and identifies emerging trends before they peak."

Step 2 — Identify the emotional benefit.
What does the user feel when they use this product effectively? Confident? Informed? In control? Ahead?

Step 3 — Find the metaphor.
What single image or concept captures both the product function and the emotional benefit?
For intelligence/foresight: the eye that sees what others miss, the radar, the pattern in the noise.
For speed/efficiency: flow state, frictionless movement, unblocked momentum.
For connection: threads linking distributed points, signal emerging from static.
For creation: craft, the building of something, layers coming together.

Step 4 — Test the metaphor.
Can you draw the metaphor? If the metaphor is too abstract to visualize, it will not work as imagery.
Does the metaphor communicate the product without the product's name? If someone saw the hero image alone, what would they think the product does?

---

# Phase 2: Write the Story Anchors

Step 5 — Write the tagline.
The tagline expresses the metaphor and the emotional benefit in 2-6 words.
For the radar/intelligence metaphor: "See what's next." "Before the signal becomes noise." "Every pattern, one view."

Step 6 — Write the hero subheading.
The subheading answers: what is this product specifically, and who is it for?
"[Product] monitors [what] so [who] can [benefit] before [the problem happens]."

Step 7 — Map the section narrative.
Hero: the aspiration or the problem (emotional, high energy).
Features: the mechanism (the how — calm, clear, systematic).
Social proof: the evidence (warm, human, credible).
CTA: the return to aspiration (high energy again, invitation to experience it).

---

# Phase 3: Source Visual Assets

Step 8 — Write a visual brief for the hero image.
One paragraph describing: the subject, the mood, the color palette, the level of abstraction, the connection to the metaphor.

Step 9 — Generate variations.
Use MidJourney, Ideogram, or DALL-E. Generate at least 4 variations. The first is almost never the best.

Criteria for selection:
- Most concept-specific (least generic)
- Most unique (least likely to appear on a competitor's site)
- Best emotional alignment with the desired tone
- Most capable of working with text overlaid on top

Step 10 — Consider video conversion.
If the still image works, a subtle looping video version adds life.
Prompt for video: "Convert this still image to a subtle looping video. Motion should be gentle — [description of what moves]: [atmospheric drift / slow scanning beam / subtle data flow]. Duration: 15 seconds. No dramatic motion."

Step 11 — Create the mobile fallback.
Ensure the still frame of the video, or a cropped version of the image, works as a standalone visual on mobile.

---

# Phase 4: Integrate Story Into Design

Step 12 — Apply the metaphor to motion.
The hero animation should be describable as: "[Motion] that communicates [metaphor]."
"A slow scanning beam across a grid of data points that communicates pattern recognition and intelligence."

Step 13 — Apply the metaphor to color.
For intelligence/precision: dark, cool, controlled.
For warmth/connection: warm neutrals, amber accents.
For energy/speed: high contrast, saturated accent.

Step 14 — Apply the metaphor to typography.
For precision: tight letter-spacing on headlines, clinical weight choices.
For warmth: looser letter-spacing, humanist typeface.
For boldness: maximum weight, maximum scale.

Step 15 — Check for story alignment.
Read the tagline aloud. Look at the hero image. Look at the color palette. Look at the font choice.
Do they all communicate the same thing? If any element contradicts the others, it breaks the story.

---

# Phase 5: Narrative Arc Check

Step 16 — Read each section heading in order.
Does the page tell a story from hero to CTA? Can you follow the narrative thread without reading body copy?
Hero heading: establishes the aspiration or the problem.
Feature section heading: introduces the solution mechanism.
Social proof heading (if present): validates the solution.
CTA heading: restates the aspiration as an invitation.

Step 17 — Check visual energy arc.
The visual energy should be: high (hero) → medium-low (features) → medium (social proof) → high (CTA).
If the features section has the same visual energy as the hero, the arc is flat.

Step 18 — Final story test.
Remove all text mentally. Look at only the visual design.
What does this page communicate without words?
Is that answer the product's story?
