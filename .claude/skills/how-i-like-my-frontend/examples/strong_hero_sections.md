# Strong Hero Sections

Analysis of what separates premium hero sections from generic ones, with named patterns.

---

# The Left-Weighted Product Hero

Description:
Headline and CTA on the left 60%. Product UI screenshot or mockup on the right 40%. Significant vertical padding above and below. Navigation minimal and low-contrast above.

What Makes It Strong:
- Asymmetric layout breaks the centered default
- Product UI is immediately visible without scrolling
- Text reads first (natural left-to-right scan), visual confirms second
- The product is the hero element — not an abstract background

Typography:
- Headline: 64-80px, weight 800-900, tight letter-spacing (-0.02em)
- Subheading: 18-20px, weight 400, 55% opacity
- CTA: primary button with accent color, 48px+ height

Used by: Linear, Vercel, Resend, Clerk

---

# The Bold Typographic Hero

Description:
No imagery. The headline at 80-120px, extremely heavy weight, occupying most of the hero. Subtext and CTA below at dramatically smaller scale.

What Makes It Strong:
- Requires exceptional type confidence — only works with excellent typeface choices
- Immediately differentiated from the visual-heavy SaaS norm
- Works especially well for bold brands where the product itself is the statement
- Typography becomes the visual element — the ratio between display and body creates all the drama

Typography:
- Headline: 96-120px, weight 900, line-height 1.0-1.1, letter-spacing -0.03em
- Subtext: 17-19px, weight 400, dramatically smaller
- The scale difference is the design

Used by: editorial brands, agencies, confident products

---

# The Full-Bleed Concept Video Hero

Description:
Video occupies the full viewport background. Text overlay on the left with a subtle gradient from left edge toward the center for legibility. Loop is 12-15 seconds. Motion is subtle — atmospheric, not dramatic.

What Makes It Strong:
- Creates immediacy and movement without distraction
- The video is concept-specific — it communicates product metaphor through motion
- Motion is subtle enough to feel like an enhanced still image on repeat visits
- Gradient behind text maintains legibility without cards or boxes

Requirements:
- The still frame must be as strong as the video — video is an enhancement
- Motion must be directly connected to product concept
- Mobile always uses the still frame only

Used by: creative studios, high-end SaaS, concept-driven products

---

# The Split-Screen Tension Hero

Description:
A strong vertical or diagonal line divides the hero into two distinct zones. One side is text. The other is imagery. The division creates visual tension.

What Makes It Strong:
- The line or edge creates energy and movement
- Editorial feel that breaks from the standard rectangular card/image approach
- Visual and text are in clear dialogue, not just placed near each other

Variations:
- Hard edge (geometric division with different backgrounds on each half)
- Diagonal cut
- Soft bleed (text on dark left, imagery bleeds into dark from right)

---

# The Dark Centered Headline Hero

Description:
Centered headline at large scale. Dark background. One strong visual accent element (a product UI floating below the headline, or a subtle ambient glow tied to concept). CTA centered below.

What Makes It Strong:
Works as centered when the headline is exceptional and the visual accent is concept-specific.
Fails when: the accent is generic (purple gradient glow), the headline is weak, or the centering is the only decision made.

Requirements:
- Headline must be genuinely strong — this layout has nowhere to hide weak copy
- Visual accent must be concept-specific, not decorative
- Spacing must be extremely generous — centering without space reads as cramped

---

# Hero Anti-Patterns (Why Specific Designs Fail)

"Generic gradient + centered text + particle field":
Why it fails: Three default choices stacked. No concept specificity. Produces a page identical to 10,000 other AI-generated SaaS sites.

"Product UI too small to see":
Why it fails: The product visual is the most important trust signal in a product hero. If it's a thumbnail in the corner, it's not doing its job.

"Two equally prominent CTAs":
Why it fails: Hick's Law — two equal choices create hesitation. One primary (filled), one secondary (text or ghost). Never two filled buttons.

"Headline that describes the category":
Why it fails: "The best project management tool" describes a category, not a product. The headline must say something specific about this product that a competitor cannot say.
