# Frontend Psychology Rules

Users do not experience interfaces rationally. They experience them emotionally, instinctively, and within the constraints of human perception and cognition. Understanding these constraints is what separates design from decoration.

---

# The 50 Millisecond Rule

Users form first impressions of a website within 50ms of seeing it. This impression correlates strongly with whether they stay or leave.

What 50ms communicates:
- General visual quality (premium vs cheap)
- Complexity level (overwhelming vs clear)
- Familiarity (looks like something they trust)
- Emotional tone (serious vs playful, cold vs warm)

What 50ms does not communicate:
- Any of the copy
- Any specific features
- The pricing
- The company name

Rule:
The 50ms impression is determined entirely by visual design — color, contrast, layout density, typographic scale. Optimizing this impression is as important as optimizing copy.

---

# F-Pattern and Z-Pattern Scanning

F-Pattern: Users reading content-heavy pages scan in an F shape — across the top, down the left side, with diminishing attention to the right as they go further down.

Z-Pattern: Users scanning sparse layouts (landing pages) follow a Z shape — top-left to top-right, diagonally to bottom-left, across to bottom-right.

Implications:
- Place the most important information top-left or top-center
- Do not bury the primary CTA in the bottom-right of a section
- Sections with centered layouts break the natural scan path — use deliberately, not by default
- Important information on the right side of the page is seen less than information on the left

Rule:
Map the expected scan path before finalizing any layout. If the most important element is not early in the scan path, reposition it.

# Cognitive Load Reduction

Cognitive load is the mental effort required to process a page. High cognitive load causes users to leave or skip content.

Sources of high cognitive load:
- Too many visual elements competing for attention
- Too many choices (options, CTAs, navigation items)
- Inconsistent visual language (each section looks different)
- Text that requires effort to read (low contrast, poor line height, complex vocabulary)
- Animation and motion that distracts from content

Sources of low cognitive load:
- Clear visual hierarchy (one focal point per section)
- Limited palette and consistent component styling
- Generous whitespace that separates content into digestible chunks
- One primary CTA per section
- Familiar patterns used in expected ways

Rule:
When in doubt about whether to add an element, ask: does this reduce the cognitive load of understanding the product, or does it increase it? If it increases it, remove it.

# Trust Signals

Users make trust decisions about a product within the first few seconds. Design communicates trustworthiness before copy does.

Visual trust signals:
- Professional typography and visual hierarchy (polish = competence)
- Real product screenshots (demonstrates the product exists and works)
- Consistent and restrained color palette (discipline = reliability)
- Testimonials with real names, titles, and company logos
- Recognizable brand logos in a "used by" section
- Security and compliance badges where relevant

Visual trust undermining factors:
- Generic stock photography
- Spelling and grammar errors
- Inconsistent component styling
- Broken or missing hover states
- Placeholder text or "lorem ipsum" in any production context
- Images that do not load

Rule:
Every design decision sends a signal about the company behind it. A site with broken interactions signals a broken product. A site with premium craft signals a premium product.

# The Primacy and Recency Effect

Users remember the beginning and end of an experience more than the middle.

Application to landing pages:
- The hero (beginning) and the final CTA section (end) receive disproportionate memory
- Features and social proof sections (middle) should be strong but are remembered less
- The final CTA section is often neglected — it deserves as much design attention as the hero

Rule:
Treat the final CTA section with hero-level design investment. It is the last thing users see before deciding to convert or leave.

---

# Hick's Law — The Paradox of Choice

The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number of choices.

Application to CTAs:
- One primary CTA converts better than two equally weighted CTAs
- If two CTAs are needed, one must be clearly primary (filled button) and one clearly secondary (ghost/text button)
- Navigation with 8 items converts less than navigation with 5 items

Application to pricing:
- Three pricing tiers is the well-researched sweet spot
- Four or more tiers increases abandonment
- The recommended tier should be visually elevated above the others

Rule:
Every page and every section should have one primary action. More than one primary action is no primary action.

---

# Social Proof and Anchoring

Users anchor on the behavior of others when making uncertain decisions.

Types of social proof:
- Numbers: "12,000 teams trust us" (scale)
- Logos: Recognizable company logos (status transfer)
- Testimonials: Specific outcomes from named individuals (narrative credibility)
- Ratings: Star ratings (consensus signal)
- Case studies: Detailed stories with measurable results (deep credibility)

Anchoring:
- A high number shown first makes subsequent numbers feel small (good for statistics)
- Showing the most expensive plan first makes the middle plan feel reasonable
- Showing what users miss out on before showing the benefit increases conversion

Rule:
At least one social proof element must appear above the fold or within the first two sections. Social proof delayed to the bottom of the page does not counter the initial skepticism.

# Gestalt Principles in Layout

Proximity: Elements close together are perceived as related. Use this to group related information and separate unrelated sections.
Similarity: Elements that look alike are perceived as the same category. Consistent button styling groups actions together.
Continuity: The eye follows lines and curves. Use directional elements (arrows, gaze direction, leading lines) to guide attention.
Closure: The eye completes incomplete shapes. Partially visible elements at the edge of the viewport invite scrolling.

Rule:
Use partially visible content at the bottom of the viewport on long pages. It signals to users that there is more to see, increasing scroll rate.
