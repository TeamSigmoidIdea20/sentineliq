# Core Philosophy

Most successful solo startups are not revolutionary inventions.

They are:
- focused
- useful
- fast to ship
- easy to understand
- attached to real pain
- distributed effectively

The goal is not:
maximum complexity.

The goal is:
maximum usefulness with minimum execution friction.

Execution speed compounds.

Shipping creates:
- learning
- feedback
- distribution opportunities
- monetization insight
- market understanding

Products improve after launch, not before it.

# Painkiller vs Vitamin vs Candy

Always classify products into one of three categories.

---

## Painkiller

Solves:
urgent recurring pain.

Characteristics:
- users actively search for solutions
- clear ROI/value
- easier monetization
- strong retention potential

Examples:
- automation tools
- workflow tools
- debugging tools
- creator utilities
- AI productivity tools

Strongest startup category.

---

## Vitamin

Improves life but is not urgent.

Characteristics:
- useful but optional
- weaker urgency
- harder monetization

Requires:
strong differentiation or branding.

---

## Candy

Entertainment, novelty, or viral consumption.

Characteristics:
- engagement-driven
- distribution-heavy
- retention-sensitive

Examples:
- meme apps
- social experiments
- casual entertainment tools

Can scale massively but are difficult operationally.

---

# MVP Philosophy

The goal of an MVP is:
validation, not perfection.

Strong MVPs are:
- small
- focused
- understandable
- complete enough to use
- fast to iterate

Weak MVPs are:
- giant unfinished systems
- architecture experiments
- feature collections
- partially functional prototypes

---

# SLC Framework

Build products that are:

## Simple
Minimal scope.
Focused value proposition.

## Lovable
One thing noticeably better than alternatives.

Usually:
- UX
- speed
- convenience
- clarity
- workflow integration

## Complete
Actually usable end-to-end.

Includes:
- onboarding
- core workflow
- reliability
- basic polish
- monetization if relevant

Most MVPs fail because they are incomplete, not because they are too small.

# Execution Rules

---

# Scope Aggressively

Every extra feature:
- increases delay
- increases bugs
- increases complexity
- slows learning

Ask constantly:
“What is the minimum useful product?”

---

# Launch Earlier Than Comfortable

Feedback is more valuable than internal speculation.

Shipping early:
- validates demand
- reveals real usage patterns
- improves prioritization
- accelerates learning

---

# Optimize For Learning Speed

Every launch should teach:
- market demand
- user behavior
- monetization potential
- retention patterns
- distribution viability

The product is also a learning system.

---

# Prefer Workflow Integration

Strong solo products often integrate into:
existing user workflows.

Examples:
- browser extensions
- overlays
- plugins
- copilots
- automation layers

Reducing context switching increases adoption.

---

# Distribution Matters Early

Distribution is not a post-build activity.

Good founders think about:
- where users already exist
- discoverability
- organic reach
- content potential
- community relevance

before building heavily.

---
